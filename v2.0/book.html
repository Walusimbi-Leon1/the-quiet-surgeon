Chapter 2 — The Crash

The page went off at 2:14 AM. I know the time because I looked at the wall clock in the break room while I was pouring the last of the coffee from the pot that had been sitting since nine. The second hand was just passing the twelve. Then the overhead speaker crackled and said my name, and the coffee went cold in my hand.

Trauma bay two. GSW to the chest. ETA four minutes.

I set the cup down on the counter and walked. That's the thing people don't understand about trauma surgery, the part they never show in the films. There is no running. Running makes you shake. Running makes you forget where your hands are. You walk, and you let the walk settle you, and by the time you push through the double doors you are already somewhere else. You are already in the chest.

The charge nurse, Maggie, met me at the door of the bay. She's been here longer than I have, which is saying something. I've been at St. Jude's for eleven years, and Maggie has been here for nineteen. She handed me a pair of gloves without looking at me, the way you'd hand a friend a wrench.

"Male, twenty-seven, single GSW to the left anterior chest. Self-inflicted, from the look of it, but the cops haven't confirmed. The girlfriend called it in. He's conscious but fading. Pressure's eighty over palp."

"What's his name?"

"Marcus. Marcus Reed."

I nodded and stepped into the bay. The paramedics were already transferring him from their gurney to ours. He was a big guy, maybe six-three, with the kind of arms that said he worked with them. His face was pale beneath the dark skin, and his eyes were open, moving around the room the way eyes move when the brain is trying to figure out if this is real. He was talking, but not to anyone. Just sounds.

"Marcus," I said. "My name is Dr. Leon. I'm going to take care of you."

He tried to say something back. The words came out wet.

I pulled up his shirt. The wound was small, almost neat. A single entry point below the left nipple, no exit. The blood was coming out steady, not spraying, which meant the heart might still be intact, but something inside was torn. The lung, most likely. Maybe the subclavian. Maybe something worse.

"Get me a chest tube," I said. "Let's get a look."

Someone handed me the scalpel. I made the incision along the fifth intercostal space, mid-axillary line. The blade went in clean. The chest tube followed. Blood came up the tube, dark and thick, and I noted the volume, the way you note everything even when your hands are busy. About four hundred mils. That's a lot. But it wasn't flooding.

"Pressure?"

"Eighty-two over sixty. Dropping."

"Type and cross him. Two units, then we'll see. Call the OR, tell them we're coming up in ten."

I was talking to the room, but I was watching his face. Marcus was looking at the ceiling now, and his eyes had that far-off quality I know too well. The one that says he's leaving, or thinking about leaving. I couldn't tell which.

"Marcus. Stay with me. Keep your eyes on me."

He blinked. For a second, I had him. Then the monitor beeped, and I looked up at the waveform, and the rhythm was wrong. Sinus tach, then a run of PVCs. His heart was getting irritable. That's never good.

"Get me sodium bicarb. Let's push it now."

I had my hand on his chest, feeling for the thready pulse, when the doors slammed open and the surgeon on call came in. Dr. Patel. She's good. She's better than good. She took one look at the room and stepped up to the head of the bed, and I saw her eyes take in the monitor, the blood in the tube, the patient's face. She didn't ask questions. She just said, "What do we have?"

I gave her the story in forty-five seconds. She nodded, and then she was in it with me, and the room moved into that rhythm that happens when two people who know what they're doing work on the same body. No words needed. Just hands.

We got him stable enough to move. The OR was ready by the time we pushed him through the doors. I scrubbed while Patel talked to the anesthesiologist. The water was cold. It always is, no matter what they set the thermostat to. I scrubbed for the full five minutes, even though we were in a hurry. You don't skip the scrub. The hurry is exactly when you don't skip it.

In the OR, the lights were bright and the room was cold and Marcus was lying there with his chest open, and I did what I always do. I looked at the body. Not the patient. The body. The patient is somewhere else, under the anesthesia, inside the sleep. The body is just a machine, and the machine was broken.

The bullet had nicked the left internal mammary artery. It had also grazed the pericardium, just enough to cause a small effusion. The lung was collapsed, but the tissue was intact. If we could fix the artery and drain the sac, he'd make it.

"Clamp."

Patel handed me the instrument. I closed the vessel, tied it off, and watched the bleeding stop. The heart was visible now, moving under the thin layer of pericardium. It was a good heart. Strong. Beating the way hearts beat when they're not done yet.

I looked up at the clock. We were twenty-two minutes in.

"We're going to close him," I said. "Dr. Patel, can you get the chest tube positioned?"

She nodded and moved around to the other side. We worked together, pulling the wires tight, sewing the ribs together, closing the layers the way you close a book. The anesthesiologist gave him more blood. The pressure started to come up. By the time we had the skin closed, his pressure was one-ten over seventy, and I knew he was going to live.

I didn't feel anything. I never do in that moment. The relief comes later, or it doesn't come at all. Right then, I just closed the wound and stepped back and let the nurses do their work.

The quiet came after. It always does. The OR empties out, the patient goes to the ICU, and the surgeon is left standing in the hallway with the fluorescent lights humming overhead and the floor sticky under the soles of your shoes. You walk back to the locker room, and you take off the scrubs, and you stand in the shower until the water runs cold.

I don't remember the shower. I remember the hallway.

The ICU was on the third floor. I found a chair in the corner, near the window that looks out over the parking lot. The city was out there, the lights of it, the late-night traffic, the people who were sleeping while Marcus Reed was waking up. I sat in the chair and watched the door to his room.

Maggie came by at some point. She didn't say anything. She just handed me a cup of coffee, the good kind, from the machine in the break room that nobody uses because it's too slow. She must have waited for it.

"Thanks," I said.

"He's asking for his girlfriend."

"She here?"

"Waiting room. Been there since they brought him in. She's got a bruise on her arm, but she won't say anything about it."

I nodded. That was a story for another time. Or maybe it wasn't my story at all. I looked at the door to Marcus's room, and I thought about the bullet and the artery and the way he'd looked at the ceiling before the anesthesia took him. I thought about the question I always ask but never get answered: what makes a man put a gun to his own chest at two in the morning, and then change his mind halfway through?

"Let her in," I said. "Just for a few minutes."

Maggie nodded and walked away. I heard her shoes on the linoleum, the soft squeak of the rubber soles. Then the quiet came back.

I sat in the chair for a long time. The coffee went cold in my hand, and I didn't drink it. I watched the door to Marcus's room, and I thought about the other ones. The ones who didn't make it. The ones whose hearts kept beating but whose brains had already given up. The ones whose families I had to talk to, standing in the same hallway, trying to find words that don't exist.

There was a boy last week. Nineteen years old. Motorcycle accident. He'd been wearing a helmet, but the helmet doesn't matter when the truck runs a red light and takes you out at sixty miles an hour. We worked on him for four hours. We got the bleeding under control. We got the fractures fixed. We got him to the ICU.

He died at 6:40 the next morning. I was in the break room, drinking the bad coffee, when the page came through. By the time I got up there, his mother was already in the room, holding his hand. The nurses had let her in early, even though visitation hadn't started. I stood in the doorway and watched her. She wasn't crying. She was just holding his hand, the way you'd hold something that might break if you let go.

I didn't go in. There was nothing to say. The machines had already said it.

I finished the cold coffee and threw the cup in the trash. The clock on the wall said 5:47. The night was almost over. The day shift would be coming in soon, and I would hand over the floor and walk out into the morning and try to sleep, and I would wake up at four in the afternoon and do it all again.

I stood up and walked to Marcus's room. He was awake. He looked at me when I came in, and I saw the recognition in his eyes. The kind of recognition that comes from remembering something you'd rather forget.

"You're alive," I said.

He nodded. His throat was dry, his voice cracked when he spoke. "That was the plan. I guess."

"Good plan."

He almost smiled. Almost. Then his eyes moved to the door, and I knew he was looking for her. The girl with the bruise on her arm.

"She's here," I said. "She's in the waiting room. I can send her in."

He didn't say anything for a long moment. Then he closed his eyes, and I saw the tears come out from under his lids, one on each side, running down his temples into his hair.

"Tell her I'm sorry," he said.

I stood there with my hand on the doorframe and my eyes on the floor. I didn't know what to say to that. I'm a surgeon. I fix the body. The rest of it is beyond me.

"I'll send her in," I said.

I walked out of the room and down the hall. The lights were humming. The floor was sticky under my shoes. The city was waking up outside the windows, the first gray light of morning starting to crawl over the rooftops.

In the waiting room, the girl was sitting on the edge of a plastic chair, holding a cup of vending machine coffee she wasn't drinking. She stood up when she saw me.

"He's asking for you," I said.

She didn't ask if he was okay. She didn't ask anything. She just nodded and walked past me, and I saw her hand reach up and wipe at her face, and then she was gone through the door, and I was alone in the hallway with the morning coming in through the windows.

I stood there for a long moment. Then I walked to the locker room, changed out of my scrubs, and went home.
